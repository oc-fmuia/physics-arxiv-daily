### Post-inflationary axion constraints from the Lyman-$α$ forest

**arXiv ID:** 2603.04401v1
**Authors:** Olga Garcia-Gallego, Vid Iršič, Matteo Viel

Axions and axion-like particles are popular dark matter candidates, but different production histories leave different “fingerprints” in how matter clumps in the Universe. If these particles are created after inflation (the early burst of rapid expansion), they can generate isocurvature perturbations—extra fluctuations in the dark matter density that are not tied to the usual fluctuations in radiation and ordinary matter. Observations already limit such isocurvature patterns using the cosmic microwave background, but smaller-scale probes are needed because some axion scenarios mainly affect relatively small cosmic scales. This paper targets that gap by using the Lyman-α forest, the pattern of hydrogen absorption lines in quasar spectra that traces the distribution of matter on small scales.

The key innovation is an updated, simulation-based mapping between axion-induced isocurvature and what the Lyman-α forest would look like. The authors use new high-resolution hydrodynamic simulations from the Sherwood-Relics suite to predict the Lyman-α flux power spectrum (a statistical measure of how absorption varies with scale) for different levels of isocurvature. They then fit these predictions to modern, high-quality Lyman-α data to constrain f_iso, the fraction of the total primordial fluctuation power coming from isocurvature rather than the standard “adiabatic” fluctuations. A major methodological point is carefully marginalizing over astrophysical uncertainties (like the thermal state of intergalactic gas) so the constraint really targets the primordial signal rather than messy late-time physics.

They find tighter upper limits on f_iso than previous Lyman-α analyses, meaning the data allow only a small contribution from post-inflationary axion-induced isocurvature on the scales the forest probes. In practical terms, this shrinks the viable parameter space for post-inflationary axion-like dark matter models that would otherwise add extra small-scale clustering (or alter it in a distinctive way). The result strengthens the case that if axion-like particles make up dark matter, their production mechanism and initial conditions must be such that they do not inject large independent dark-matter-only fluctuations. More broadly, it reinforces the Lyman-α forest as one of the most powerful tools for testing dark matter physics that is invisible to the cosmic microwave background alone.

### Exploring gas thermodynamics around galaxies from the Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effects: impact of galaxy-halo connection, 2D projection and velocity field

**arXiv ID:** 2603.04397v1
**Authors:** Sadaf Kadir, Bernardita Ried Guachalla, Sihan Yuan

In the last decade, astronomers have realized that the faint distortions galaxies imprint on the cosmic microwave background (the leftover light from the Big Bang) can be used to “weigh” and “take the temperature” of the gas in and around their dark-matter halos. The thermal Sunyaev–Zel’dovich effect (tSZ) traces how hot and pressurized the gas is, while the kinematic SZ effect (kSZ) traces how much gas is moving toward or away from us, but turning those signals into a clear physical picture is hard. The main difficulty is that real observations mix together many halos along the line of sight, and the link between a galaxy you see and the mass of the halo it lives in (the “galaxy–halo connection”) is uncertain. This paper targets those obstacles to show how they bias, blur, or masquerade as genuine gas-physics signals.

The authors build a framework that treats tSZ, kSZ, and the relativistic SZ (rSZ, which is sensitive to very hot electrons) as a combined thermodynamics toolkit rather than separate measurements. They explicitly model the 2D projection problem: what you observe is a stacked, line-of-sight sum of gas from the target halo plus neighboring structures, which can change the apparent profile of pressure and temperature around galaxies. They also incorporate the velocity field more realistically for kSZ, emphasizing that coherent bulk flows and their correlations can alter the inferred gas distribution if they’re simplified or ignored. A central innovation is to systematically vary assumptions about the galaxy–halo connection and show how those assumptions propagate into the SZ-derived gas properties.

They find that uncertainties in how galaxies populate halos, plus unavoidable projection effects, can significantly distort the SZ signals in ways that could be misread as evidence for stronger or weaker feedback (energy injection from stars and black holes) than is actually present. In particular, the paper argues that the observed 2D SZ patterns are not a clean map of a single halo’s gas, but a blended imprint of the halo, its environment, and the surrounding velocity flows, which is especially consequential for kSZ. By analyzing tSZ, kSZ, and rSZ together within the same modeling framework, they show a path to disentangle gas temperature, density, and motion more reliably and to reduce systematic biases. The broader impact is a clearer roadmap for using CMB-based SZ measurements to test galaxy formation models and to improve predictions for related cosmological probes, such as how gas affects gravitational lensing measurements.

If axions are produced after inflation (the rapid early expansion), they can generate extra “isocurvature” fluctuations—variations in the dark matter component that are not matched by the ordinary matter and radiation—and these can subtly change how structure forms on small scales. The Lyman‑α forest, a pattern of absorption lines in quasar spectra caused by intergalactic hydrogen, is one of the sharpest tools we have to test small-scale clustering at redshifts where the Universe was a few billion years old. This paper targets the problem of turning Lyman‑α measurements into updated, robust limits on how large the axion-induced isocurvature contribution could be.

The key methodological step is to model how post‑inflationary axion-like particles would modify the matter power spectrum (a statistic describing how “clumpy” matter is as a function of scale) and then propagate that change through realistic simulations of the intergalactic medium. The authors use new hydrodynamical simulations from the Sherwood‑Relics suite, which are designed to predict the Lyman‑α flux power spectrum with controlled astrophysical systematics (like the thermal history of the gas). They then fit the simulated predictions to high‑resolution Lyman‑α forest data to infer the allowed isocurvature fraction f_iso, which quantifies how much of the total clustering signal could come from these axion-induced isocurvature fluctuations. In essence, they update the “translation” between axion microphysics and an observable signature in quasar absorption spectra using a newer simulation framework.

They find updated constraints that limit f_iso to be small, meaning there is little room for post‑inflationary axion-induced isocurvature to contribute significantly to the observed clustering on Lyman‑α scales. Practically, this tightens the viable parameter space for post‑inflationary ALP dark matter scenarios that would otherwise predict extra small‑scale structure compared with standard cold dark matter. The impact is to strengthen the role of Lyman‑α data as a complementary probe to the cosmic microwave background, especially because it is sensitive to much smaller scales where these effects can be more pronounced. Overall, the results push axion models toward behaving more like standard cold dark matter in their perturbation signatures, or else require model details that suppress the problematic isocurvature component.

### Non-Hermitian Quantum Mechanics with Applications to Gravity

**arXiv ID:** 2603.04375v1
**Authors:** Oem Trivedi, Alfredo Gurrola, Robert J. Scherrer

In standard quantum mechanics, operators are assumed to be “Hermitian,” which is a mathematical condition that guarantees measurable quantities come out real and that probabilities add up to one as a system evolves. Over the last couple of decades, researchers have explored “non-Hermitian” quantum theories that can still behave sensibly when they have hidden symmetries or use a modified notion of probability, but it has remained unclear when non-Hermiticity is a physical feature versus a sign of an inconsistent setup. Gravity and curved spacetime make this issue sharper because defining “probability conservation” depends on how you slice spacetime into moments of time (Cauchy surfaces) and on what can leak through boundaries like horizons. This paper targets that gap: it asks whether Hermiticity should be treated as a basic axiom, or instead as something that emerges from a deeper conservation law that may fail or be altered in gravitational settings.

The central new idea is to reinterpret Hermiticity as a symmetry requirement tied to a conserved “inner product current,” essentially a flow that encodes conservation of the quantum inner product (the rule that gives probabilities). Instead of starting with the usual textbook inner product and demanding the Hamiltonian be Hermitian, the authors start with a spacetime-based conservation statement and derive when the familiar Hermiticity condition follows. They emphasize the role of complete Cauchy surfaces (surfaces that capture all information needed to predict the future) and analyze what happens when there is flux through boundaries, meaning probability can effectively pass through a causal horizon or a boundary of the region you track. In those cases, the usual Hermiticity condition is no longer the right symmetry statement, and the correct description naturally becomes non-Hermitian from the viewpoint of the restricted region.

They find that in “nice” spacetimes where you can choose complete Cauchy surfaces and there is no boundary leakage, conservation of the inner product current reduces to ordinary Hermiticity and standard unitary time evolution. But when there are causal horizons or boundary flux, the same conservation principle leads to effective non-Hermitian evolution for the subsystem you can access, reflecting real loss or gain of probability due to information crossing the boundary. This reframes non-Hermitian quantum mechanics not as a mathematical oddity, but as an expected effective description whenever gravity or causal structure prevents you from accounting for all degrees of freedom. The impact is conceptual: it suggests Hermiticity is not a universal axiom, but a contingent symmetry that holds when spacetime lets you close the accounting of probability, and it gives a principled way to connect non-Hermitian dynamics to gravitational phenomena like horizons.

If axions were produced after inflation (the rapid early expansion), they can naturally generate this kind of composition ripple, which then affects the later distribution of gas. The problem the paper tackles is how strongly current observations of the Lyman-α forest—the pattern of hydrogen absorption lines in quasar light that maps small-scale structure—can limit the allowed size of this isocurvature contribution.

They frame the axion effect through an “isocurvature fraction,” f_iso, which tells you what share of the total matter-fluctuation power comes from axion-induced isocurvature rather than the usual “adiabatic” fluctuations measured in the cosmic microwave background. By generating synthetic Lyman-α spectra across a range of axion isocurvature levels and comparing them to high-resolution data, they translate observed small-scale clustering into bounds on f_iso. This approach leverages the Lyman-α forest as a precision small-scale probe that is complementary to the cosmic microwave background, which is most sensitive to larger scales.

They find that Lyman-α data place strengthened, updated upper limits on the allowed isocurvature fraction from post-inflationary axion-like particles, meaning large axion-induced composition ripples are disfavored. In practical terms, the small-scale structure implied by the observed forest does not tolerate much extra “nonstandard” power from axion isocurvature without spoiling the match to the data. This tightens the viable parameter space for post-inflationary axion/ALP dark matter scenarios and clarifies how much room is left for axions to be all (or a large fraction) of dark matter in models that predict significant isocurvature. More broadly, it reinforces the Lyman-α forest as one of the most sensitive astrophysical tools for testing dark matter microphysics that only shows up on small scales.

### A framework for missing-energy searches with anomalous light vectors

**arXiv ID:** 2603.04394v1
**Authors:** Luca Di Luzio, Marco Nardecchia, Stefano Scacco

Light “dark” spin-1 particles (new force carriers often called Z′ bosons) are a popular target in missing-energy experiments, where something invisible is produced and inferred from momentum imbalance. Many studies assume these particles couple in simple, anomaly-free ways to known particles, so the theory remains mathematically consistent at high energies. But if the new particle couples to certain combinations of Standard Model currents that are “anomalous” (they break a quantum consistency condition tied to gauge symmetry), the theory must be completed by adding new charged fermions, and that completion can strongly affect experimental signals. This paper addresses how to build a reliable low-energy description of such anomalous light vectors that experiments can use without committing to a detailed, model-by-model ultraviolet (high-energy) theory.

The key idea is to treat the needed new fermions (“anomalons”) systematically and then integrate them out to obtain a consistent effective theory valid at accessible energies. When these anomalons get their mass from the same symmetry breaking that gives the light vector its mass, the low-energy theory inevitably contains special interaction terms (Wess–Zumino terms) whose strengths are fixed by anomaly matching, meaning the low-energy interactions must reproduce the same anomaly structure as the full theory. The authors classify “minimal” anomalon spectra: the smallest sets of new fermions with electroweak charges that cancel the anomalies for generic charge assignments. They then build a framework for missing-energy searches that keeps these forced, anomaly-fixed interactions rather than ignoring them.

They find that, once consistency is enforced, the infrared (low-energy) behavior of anomalous light vectors is not arbitrary: it comes with predictive couplings to electroweak gauge bosons (the W, Z, and photon) dictated by the mixed anomalies. This changes how missing-energy signals should be modeled, because production and decay channels can be dominated or reshaped by these anomaly-induced interactions, and some channels that look suppressed in naive models can become important. The classification of minimal anomalon content also clarifies what kinds of new charged states must exist in the UV completion and how heavy they can be while still leaving observable footprints. Overall, the paper tightens the connection between theoretical consistency and experimental strategy, providing a more trustworthy roadmap for interpreting (or ruling out) light-vector missing-energy scenarios.

### Gravitational confinement of ghost scalar fields in neutron stars

**arXiv ID:** 2603.04400v1
**Authors:** Argelia Bernal, Víctor Jaramillo, Néstor A. Montiel-Hernández

Neutron stars are so dense that their gravity can trap not only ordinary matter but also hypothetical new fields, making them natural laboratories for testing exotic physics. One particularly speculative ingredient is a “ghost” scalar field, meaning a field whose kinetic energy has the opposite sign from normal, which often leads to instabilities and makes it unclear whether such matter could exist in a long-lived object. Previous work has studied exotic fields around compact stars or black holes, but the key open problem is whether a neutron star could hold a nonzero amount of ghost matter without immediately destabilizing or destroying the star. This paper targets that question by asking: can gravity confine ghost scalar matter inside neutron-star cores, and if so, is it stable under time evolution?

The authors build models where ordinary neutron-star fluid is coupled to gravity and to a complex ghost scalar field (a scalar with two components, often used because it can form long-lived, bound configurations). They first compute static, equilibrium solutions of the full coupled system—essentially “candidate neutron stars” that already include a trapped ghost component. Then they test those equilibria with fully nonlinear numerical simulations in time, letting the combined system evolve to see whether it settles down, oscillates, or runs away to a catastrophic outcome. This combination of constructing equilibria and then directly evolving them is important because ghost fields can look acceptable on paper but fail dynamically.

They find that neutron stars can, in fact, gravitationally confine a finite amount of ghost scalar matter, forming continuous families of mixed configurations rather than a single fine-tuned case. Their dynamical evolutions show that some of these configurations can remain well-behaved over time (typically by settling into oscillations), while others become unstable once the ghost component is too large or arranged unfavorably. The takeaway is that “ghostlike” ingredients are not automatically excluded by immediate collapse or explosion when placed in a realistic neutron-star environment; instead, there are thresholds and stability windows set by gravity and the star’s structure. This reshapes the discussion from “ghost fields are instantly pathological” to “if they existed, compact stars could hide limited amounts of them, and stability becomes an empirical question tied to neutron-star observations and simulations.”

### Non-Hermitian Quantum Mechanics with Applications to Gravity

**arXiv ID:** 2603.04375v1
**Authors:** Oem Trivedi, Alfredo Gurrola, Robert J. Scherrer

In standard quantum mechanics, we usually assume the Hamiltonian (the operator that governs time evolution) is Hermitian, which is the textbook condition that guarantees measurable energies are real and that probabilities add up to one over time. Over the last two decades, researchers have learned that some “non-Hermitian” systems can still behave consistently if you modify what you mean by the inner product (the rule for turning wavefunctions into probabilities), but this is often treated as a special trick rather than something fundamental. Gravity and curved spacetime complicate matters further because spacetime can have horizons or regions you cannot access, so the usual arguments for probability conservation can fail in subtle ways. This paper addresses that gap: it asks whether Hermiticity should really be an axiom, or whether it emerges only when spacetime allows global probability conservation with no leakage.

The central new idea is to treat Hermiticity as a symmetry principle tied to the conservation of an “inner product current,” a relativistic way of tracking probability flow through spacetime. When this current is globally conserved—meaning you can choose complete “time slices” (Cauchy surfaces) and no probability flows out through boundaries—this symmetry reduces to the familiar Hermiticity condition using the standard quantum inner product. But if spacetime has causal horizons or boundary flux (probability can disappear behind a horizon or leave the region you describe), the usual Hermiticity condition need not hold, and the correct framework becomes explicitly non-Hermitian. The paper’s method is to derive the appropriate quantum evolution rules directly from these geometric conservation laws rather than assuming a fixed Hilbert-space structure from the start.

They find that what looks like non-Hermitian quantum mechanics can naturally arise in gravitational settings not because quantum theory is breaking, but because the observer’s accessible region of spacetime does not support a globally conserved inner product. In “nice” spacetimes with complete slices and no flux, the theory collapses back to ordinary unitary quantum mechanics, explaining why Hermiticity works so well in familiar situations. In spacetimes with horizons or effective boundaries, the evolution can be non-unitary from the local perspective, reflecting real probability loss or gain through regions you cannot track, while still being governed by a deeper conservation law in the full spacetime picture. The impact is a conceptual shift: instead of treating Hermiticity as a sacred starting point, the paper reframes it as an emergent condition whose validity depends on spacetime structure, which could clarify how quantum theory should be formulated in gravitational and horizon-rich environments.
