### Post-inflationary axion constraints from the Lyman-$α$ forest

**arXiv ID:** 2603.04401v1
**Authors:** Olga Garcia-Gallego, Vid Iršič, Matteo Viel

Axions and axion-like particles are leading dark-matter candidates, and a key question is whether their production in the early Universe leaves observable “extra” fluctuations beyond the standard pattern seeded by inflation. If axions are created after inflation (the “post-inflationary” scenario), they can generate isocurvature perturbations—density variations that are initially out of step with the usual matter-radiation fluctuations—which would subtly reshape the distribution of matter on small cosmic scales. The Lyman-α forest, a set of absorption features in quasar light caused by intergalactic hydrogen gas, is one of the best probes of those small scales at high redshift. This paper targets the problem of translating high-quality Lyman-α measurements into updated, robust limits on how large the axion-induced isocurvature contribution can be.

The authors introduce updated constraints by using new dedicated numerical simulations from the Sherwood-Relics suite that are designed to model how different small-scale power spectra propagate into the Lyman-α forest signal. Instead of only comparing to generic “warm dark matter-like” suppression, they focus on an explicit parameter, the isocurvature fraction f_iso, which quantifies how much of the total power spectrum comes from axion-induced isocurvature modes. They fit high-resolution Lyman-α forest data with these simulations, while accounting for astrophysical effects that can mimic or hide small-scale changes (for example, uncertainties in the intergalactic gas temperature and its evolution). Methodologically, the novelty is in the improved mapping between axion-motivated initial fluctuations and the observable Lyman-α flux statistics using an up-to-date simulation framework.

They find updated upper limits on f_iso, tightening how much post-inflationary axion-like isocurvature power can be present without conflicting with the observed fine-grained structure seen in the Lyman-α forest. In practical terms, the data prefer a Universe whose small-scale matter clustering is close to the standard cold-dark-matter expectation, leaving limited room for additional axion-induced fluctuations. This strengthens the case that if axion-like particles make up dark matter in a post-inflationary scenario, their associated isocurvature imprint must be small, pushing viable models into a narrower region of parameter space. More broadly, the work reinforces the Lyman-α forest as a precision tool for testing not just the amount of dark matter, but the detailed way it was produced in the early Universe.

### Exploring gas thermodynamics around galaxies from the Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effects: impact of galaxy-halo connection, 2D projection and velocity field

**arXiv ID:** 2603.04397v1
**Authors:** Sadaf Kadir, Bernardita Ried Guachalla, Sihan Yuan

Astronomers know that most of a galaxy’s ordinary matter (gas) is not in stars but in a diffuse “atmosphere” around the galaxy, and that this gas is heated, pushed around, and sometimes expelled by processes like supernovae and black-hole activity (often called feedback). A powerful way to study this otherwise faint gas is to look for tiny changes it makes to the cosmic microwave background (CMB): the thermal Sunyaev–Zel’dovich effect (tSZ) from hot electrons, and the kinematic SZ effect (kSZ) from gas moving toward or away from us. Recent studies have used these signals to estimate average gas pressure and motion around galaxies, but the interpretation is messy because observed signals are projected along the line of sight and depend on how galaxies occupy dark-matter halos (the “galaxy–halo connection”). This paper tackles how those modeling choices and projection effects can bias what we infer about gas temperature, density, and velocity around galaxies.

The authors build a framework that treats the three SZ signals together: tSZ (mostly sensitive to pressure), kSZ (sensitive to the amount of free electrons times their bulk velocity), and the relativistic SZ correction (rSZ), which adds sensitivity to electron temperature beyond the standard tSZ. They explicitly model the galaxy–halo connection, meaning they account for the fact that a given galaxy sample can live in a mix of halo masses and environments, not a single “typical” halo. They also focus on realistic 2D projection, showing how gas at different distances along the same line of sight and correlated nearby structures can contaminate the signal. Finally, they incorporate the gas velocity field in a way that links kSZ measurements to coherent motions and gravitational potentials, rather than treating velocities as an unstructured nuisance.

They find that combining tSZ, kSZ, and rSZ can, in principle, separate key gas properties that are otherwise tangled together, but only if the galaxy–halo connection and projection effects are modeled carefully. In particular, mismodeling the halo-mass distribution of the galaxy sample or ignoring correlated material along the line of sight can mimic changes in gas temperature or pressure, leading to biased conclusions about feedback strength. They show that velocity information in kSZ adds complementary leverage because it responds differently to projection than temperature-based signals, helping break degeneracies (cases where different physical scenarios look similar in the data). Overall, the work sharpens how we can translate upcoming high-precision CMB measurements into reliable statements about where galaxies keep their gas, how hot it is, and how feedback and gravity shape it.

### Non-Hermitian Quantum Mechanics with Applications to Gravity

**arXiv ID:** 2603.04375v1
**Authors:** Oem Trivedi, Alfredo Gurrola, Robert J. Scherrer

In standard quantum mechanics, we usually demand that the Hamiltonian (the operator that generates time evolution) is “Hermitian,” a mathematical property that guarantees measurable energies are real and probabilities add up to one over time (unitary evolution). Over the last few decades, researchers have explored broader “non-Hermitian” models that can still behave sensibly if you change how you define the inner product (the rule for computing probabilities), and these ideas show up naturally in open systems, effective theories, and wave physics. Gravity adds another wrinkle because spacetime can have horizons (one-way causal boundaries) or singularities, and then it is less obvious which parts of a wave can influence which others and whether probability can “leak” out of the region you can describe. This paper targets that mismatch: it asks whether Hermiticity should be an axiom, or whether it should emerge only when spacetime geometry guarantees a certain kind of probability conservation.

The key conceptual move is to reinterpret Hermiticity not as a starting rule but as a symmetry-like consequence of a globally conserved “inner product current,” essentially a probability-flow bookkeeping law in spacetime. The authors emphasize that when spacetime admits complete Cauchy surfaces (slices of spacetime that capture all information needed to predict the future) and there is no flux through boundaries, that conservation law reduces to the usual textbook Hermiticity condition for the standard inner product. But if there are causal horizons or other places where information can flow out of the domain you track, the same conservation logic instead points to an effectively non-Hermitian description for the remaining degrees of freedom. Methodologically, the paper reframes the discussion in geometric terms—what regions of spacetime you can close off and whether probability current crosses their boundaries—rather than treating non-Hermiticity as an ad hoc modification.

They find that whether quantum evolution looks Hermitian is tied to global spacetime structure: in “closed” situations with no probability leakage, standard Hermitian quantum mechanics is recovered, while in geometries with horizons or other causal obstructions, non-Hermitian evolution can naturally arise for an observer restricted to an accessible region. In that sense, apparent non-unitarity is not necessarily a breakdown of quantum mechanics, but a signal that probability is flowing through boundaries your description does not include. This shifts the interpretation of non-Hermitian models in gravitational settings from being exotic alternatives to being effective, geometry-driven descriptions. The broader impact is a clearer bridge between quantum foundations and gravitational environments: what looks like a fundamental axiom (Hermiticity) may instead be contingent on global conservation conditions that gravity can obstruct.

## High-Energy Physics (Phenomenology)

### A framework for missing-energy searches with anomalous light vectors

**arXiv ID:** 2603.04394v1
**Authors:** Luca Di Luzio, Marco Nardecchia, Stefano Scacco

Light “dark” force carriers (new spin-1 particles often called Z′ bosons) are widely studied because they could connect ordinary matter to hidden sectors and show up as missing energy at colliders or fixed-target experiments. Many well-known cases avoid theoretical pitfalls by coupling the new boson to currents that are automatically consistent with the Standard Model, but more general couplings can run into “anomalies,” a quantum inconsistency where gauge symmetry would fail unless extra particles exist. In practice, this means that an apparently simple low-energy model for a light vector boson may be incomplete or even misleading about what signals to expect. This paper targets that gap: how to build and use a consistent, low-energy framework for missing-energy searches when the new light vector couples to currents that are anomalous with respect to electroweak interactions.

The key idea is to treat anomaly cancellation and its low-energy fingerprints in a systematic way. The authors emphasize that generic charge choices require new fermions (“anomalons”) that carry electroweak charges and are chiral under the new U(1) symmetry, and if these fermions get mass from the same symmetry breaking that gives the new boson its mass, then removing them from the low-energy theory leaves behind specific residual interactions. Those residual interactions are Wess–Zumino terms: special, symmetry-required couplings between the new vector and Standard Model gauge fields whose strengths are fixed by anomaly matching (the requirement that the low-energy theory reproduces the same anomaly structure as the full theory). They then classify minimal sets of anomalons that can cancel the mixed anomalies and derive the corresponding effective interactions that experiments should use.

They find that once you enforce consistency, missing-energy signals are not just controlled by the direct coupling of the new boson to Standard Model matter, but also by unavoidable interactions involving the electroweak gauge bosons that can open new production and decay channels. In other words, even if the anomalons are too heavy to produce directly, their existence leaves an experimentally relevant imprint through fixed, model-independent couplings in the infrared (low-energy) description. This reshapes how one should interpret constraints and projections: some regions of parameter space that look viable in “simplified” models are altered when the anomaly-matched Wess–Zumino interactions are included, and complementary search modes become important. Overall, the paper provides a more reliable template for designing and comparing missing-energy searches for light vectors in theoretically consistent scenarios.

## General Relativity & Quantum Cosmology

### Gravitational confinement of ghost scalar fields in neutron stars

**arXiv ID:** 2603.04400v1
**Authors:** Argelia Bernal, Víctor Jaramillo, Néstor A. Montiel-Hernández

Neutron stars are extreme gravity laboratories where theorists test how matter behaves when squeezed beyond what we can reproduce on Earth. A long-standing “no-go” issue is that many exotic fields, especially “ghost” fields with the wrong sign of kinetic energy, are usually unstable: small disturbances can grow instead of settling down. Even so, these fields appear in some speculative modifications of gravity and cosmology, so it matters to know whether ordinary astrophysical objects could trap or amplify them. This paper asks a focused question: can the strong gravity and dense fluid of a neutron star confine a finite amount of ghost scalar matter in its core without immediately destroying the star or triggering runaway instabilities?

The authors build a model where a normal neutron-star fluid (described by standard relativistic hydrodynamics) is coupled to gravity and to a complex ghost scalar field (a wave-like field with negative kinetic term). They first compute static, self-consistent star-plus-field configurations by solving the combined Einstein equations (for spacetime), Euler equations (for the fluid), and Klein–Gordon equation (for the scalar field). Then they go beyond equilibrium by performing fully nonlinear time-dependent simulations, letting the coupled system evolve to test whether the configurations persist, drift, or catastrophically destabilize. This two-step approach—construct equilibrium families and then dynamically stress-test them—is the key methodological innovation for assessing real stability rather than just mathematical existence.

They find that neutron stars can, in fact, gravitationally confine a finite amount of ghost scalar matter and still form continuous families of equilibrium solutions, rather than failing immediately. In dynamical evolutions of representative cases, the confined ghost component can remain trapped for significant times, indicating that the star’s gravity can “bottle” even a potentially pathological field under some conditions. At the same time, the results map out when the system becomes unstable and how the instability develops nonlinearly, clarifying that confinement does not mean unconditional safety. Overall, the work sharpens our understanding of how exotic fields might hide inside compact stars and provides a concrete framework to translate “ghost field” ideas from abstract theory into astrophysically testable stability questions.
